# The Receipt Is the Institution

### Why an escrowed bounty is freedom tech

*poidh as Freedom Tech — submission for bounty #1485*

---

Most coordination technology asks you to trust an intermediary before anything happens. You join a
platform, accept its terms, hand over your identity, and hope that when the work is done the platform
agrees with you about whether it was done. The queue is the product; you are the inventory.

poidh inverts the order. The money moves first. The work moves second. Judgment moves last, and
every step leaves a receipt that anyone can read. That inversion is not a UX detail — it is the
whole political content of the thing.

## 1. Escrow is a promise that does not need a promiser

A promise normally has a half-life. It decays with the memory of the person who made it, with their
incentives, with their mood, with whether they still work at the company. "I'll pay you for that" is
worth exactly as much as the counterparty's future self.

An escrowed bounty replaces the promiser with a contract. Once the ETH is in the bounty contract,
the issuer cannot unilaterally take it back, cannot quietly decide the work was not good enough and
keep the money, cannot disappear. The funds are not a budget line that might survive a reorg; they
are a balance that a smart contract will release according to rules that were visible before anyone
started working.

This matters most for the people with the least leverage. A freelancer with a contract and a lawyer
does not need escrow — the lawyer is the escrow. A stranger in another jurisdiction with no
reputation, no references, and no ability to sue does need it. Escrow is what makes it rational for a
nobody to do work for somebody they have never met. That is not a convenience. That is the difference
between a closed guild and an open market.

## 2. Permissionlessness is about who may *define* the work, not only who may do it

It is easy to read "permissionless" as "anyone can submit." That is the weaker half. The stronger half
is that anyone can *post*: anyone can put money behind an outcome they want to see in the world and
invite the entire internet to compete for it.

Think about what it costs to commission something in a normal institution. You need a legal entity, a
procurement process, a vendor list, an invoice, a bank account, a tax footprint, and someone willing to
sign. The friction is not there to protect quality; it is there because the institution cannot verify
strangers cheaply. Escrow plus public claims is a cheap verification layer. It lets a person with an
idea and a small amount of capital create a real, funded demand signal — one that strangers on the
other side of the planet can act on within minutes.

The direction of power matters. In most platforms, supply waits for demand to be routed to it by an
algorithm the supply cannot inspect. Here, demand is public, priced, and addressable directly. A
teenager in Gorontalo and a lab in Berlin see the same bounty, the same rules, and the same deadline.
Nothing about the interface asks who they are before it asks what they can do.

## 3. Proof-first culture, not trust-first culture

"Pics or it didn't happen" sounds like a joke until you notice it is a governance model.

Every claim on poidh is an assertion with evidence attached: a screenshot, a repository, a
transaction, a photograph of the thing you said you did. The claim is public before it is judged. That
ordering — publish, then adjudicate — is what separates an accountable process from a discretionary
one. When evidence is visible first, the decision can be argued with. When the decision comes first,
evidence is just decoration.

This is also why the platform's social layer is not noise. Claims are visible to everyone competing,
so quality becomes a public good: people improve their submissions because the bar is legible, and
they can see exactly how far above or below it they are.

## 4. The receipt is the institution

Institutions persist by remembering. Charters, minutes, ledgers, deeds — the point of every
bureaucratic artifact is to make a past decision enforceable in the present by someone who was not
there.

poidh is a small, working version of that idea with the middlemen deleted. A bounty is a charter. A
claim is a filing. An acceptance is a judgment. The chain is the archive. And unlike a national
archive, the archive is not the property of the state that produced it: it is readable by anyone with
a browser, and it cannot be quietly revised to make last year's promise disappear.

That is the freedom-tech claim, stripped of ideology. Not "crypto makes you free." Rather: the
ability to make a binding commitment to a stranger, to have that commitment enforced by something
other than the stranger's goodwill, and to have the record of it survive the platform, the issuer, and
the mood of the moment.

## 5. What it costs, honestly

Freedom tech is not free tech. Someone pays gas. Someone eats the risk of an issuer who never accepts
a claim. Judgment is still human and therefore still arbitrary at the edges — a creator can simply
prefer a submission you find worse, and the contract will honor that. Escrow protects the money from
being stolen; it does not protect you from being unpersuasive.

The honest case for poidh is not that it removes discretion. It is that it *localizes* discretion:
one named issuer, one visible pot, one deadline, one public set of competitors. That is a much smaller
surface for corruption than a platform that decides invisibly, at scale, which work exists.

## 6. Why this is the interesting direction

The internet's first thirty years were very good at moving information and very bad at moving
commitment. We can read anything and trust almost nothing. Every layer that solved trust did it by
becoming a gatekeeper — payment processors, marketplaces, review systems, escrow agents — and every
gatekeeper eventually discovered it could charge rent for the trust it was holding.

A protocol that escrows value, publishes the terms, and records the outcome is a way to get the
useful part of a marketplace without installing a landlord in the middle. It is not a company and it
does not want to be one. It is a ledger with a social layer, and the social layer is doing the work
that a legal department would otherwise do.

Pics or it didn't happen is, in the end, a theory of legitimacy: claims must be evidenced, evidence
must be public, and payment must follow proof rather than permission.

That is a strange thing for a bounty site to be. It is a very good thing for the internet to have.
